# MCU phase controller, rectified-DC variant: draft schematic

*Drafted by Claude at Bob Drzyzgula's direction, October 2026. Not built or
tested.*

**A concept, not a design.** Drawn but not built, simulated (beyond the
waveform model in the papers) or tested; part values are first estimates.
The motor-control paper (§11, "A rectified-DC variant") describes it.

`mcu_controller_rectified.kicad_sch` (KiCad 7) is the mains controller of
`hardware/mcu-controller/` with one change in the power path. The triac
feeds the universal motor through a bridge rectifier, with a freewheeling
diode across the motor. The motor then runs on phase-controlled DC, much
as on a KBIC, while everything else stays as it was. That includes the
isolation, the triac and its firing, the zero-cross, the relay, the supply
and the whole control side. `mcu_controller_rectified.pdf` is the sheet
printed.

The file is generated by `tools/make_rectified_schematic.py`, a copy of
`tools/make_mcu_schematic.py` with this change. It prints the netlist it
intends (`netlist.txt`), and `tools/check_mcu_netlist.py` confirms that
KiCad's own netlist export matches it.

## What changed from the mains version

- **BR1, a bridge rectifier** (GBU8J class, 600 V, 8 A), between the triac
  and the motor. One AC input comes from the triac, through the current
  transformer's window; the other goes to N.
- **D3, a freewheeling diode** across the motor's DC terminals (UF5408
  class: fast recovery, 1000 V, 3 A), cathode to DC+. When the line
  voltage reaches zero, the motor's current moves onto D3, so the motor
  never sees a reverse voltage. The triac's current falls cleanly to zero,
  and it turns off with no inductive load to fight.
- **J3, the motor's own connector.** The motor leaves the duplex outlet:
  that socket would now carry DC, and anything else plugged into it would
  get DC too. The motor's leads either pass through and are hardwired, or
  end in a keyed connector that cannot be mistaken for a mains one, such
  as a rectangular Molex Mini-Fit. J3 is at line potential and needs
  insulation to match. The outlet (J2) keeps only the light's socket,
  always on.
- **T1 stays on the AC side,** in the bridge's AC lead, because a current
  transformer cannot sense DC. It still carries the full motor current.

## What it does

The simulation in the motor-control paper, using *Inside the Motor*'s
representative motor and load, gives the main effect. The current no
longer falls to zero between firings; it ripples on a steady value. The
torque is then far less pulsed at low speed.

- **At a firing angle of 120°,** the motor runs at about 1,690 rpm, against
  about 970 rpm on AC.
- **At 135°,** where the AC version stalls, it still turns at about 390 rpm.

The firmware is unchanged in structure: the same zero-cross, firing and
loops. The gains need retuning, because the same firing angle now gives
more speed. On DC the motor also runs faster at full firing than on AC, so
the speed limit and the stall watchdog matter a little more.

## Cautions

- **The motor's connector is at line potential.** It is keyed and
  insulated, but it is not low voltage.
- **Heat.** BR1 and D3 between them carry the motor current, and BR1 drops
  about 2 V. A motor drawing an amp or so dissipates about 2 W in them,
  easily handled by the GBU package on a little copper or a small
  heatsink.
- **Never scope the line side,** as for the mains version (see the test
  rig's guide).
