# MCU phase controller: draft schematic

*Drafted by Claude at Bob Drzyzgula's direction, October 2026. Not built or
tested.*

**A concept, not a design.** Drawn but not built, simulated or tested;
part values are first estimates; the core board is drawn by GPIO number,
not as any particular board; the firmware does not exist. The motor-control
paper (§11, "A first concept circuit") describes it.

`mcu_controller.kicad_sch` (KiCad 7) is a phase controller after Freescale's
AN1662, with the control side isolated from the mains, built around a
Raspberry Pi RP2350B core board and a Hall sensor at the hand wheel. (The
first draft used an STM32 NUCLEO-G431KB; the RP2350B was chosen for its
price, its eight analog inputs and its familiarity to hobbyists.)
`mcu_controller.pdf` is the same sheet printed. The isolation barrier runs
across the sheet, with the live side above it.

The file is generated by `tools/make_mcu_schematic.py`, which defines every
symbol itself (no KiCad libraries needed), places the parts and draws the
wires from its tables; the +5V, +3V3 and GND rails use power ports. It
splits wires where another wire or a pin lands on them, adds the junction
dots, and prints the netlist it intends (`netlist.txt`).
`tools/check_mcu_netlist.py` compares that with KiCad's own netlist export;
they match. To change the circuit, edit the script's tables and rerun it
from the repository root, or open the file in KiCad and carry on by hand
(then the script no longer applies). `tools/export_schematic.sh` makes the
paper's figure from it.

## What is on the sheet

**Line side (live).** IEC inlet, T2A fuse and a 150 VAC MOV; the duplex
outlet (LIGHT always on, MOTOR controlled); a motor-enable relay (K1) whose
contact feeds the triac, so a firmware fault can always cut the motor; the
triac (BTA08-600BW, snubberless) in series with the motor, fired through a
MOC3023 random-phase opto-triac, with an RC snubber footprint left unfitted;
an H11AA1 AC-input optocoupler as the zero-cross detector; an isolated 5 V
module (Mean Well IRM-05-5 class) on its own T250mA fuse; and, optional, a
current transformer (T1, ZMCT103C class, 1000:1) whose window the motor's
hot lead passes through, for IR compensation (see the paper's "Load
compensation without a machine sensor"). Its secondary is on the control
side: a 100 Ω burden gives about 0.1 V per amp, centered on a 1.65 V bias,
filtered into ADC4.

**Control side (SELV).** The RP2350B core board, fed 5 V into VSYS from
the module through a Schottky diode (so its USB port can stay plugged in),
its own regulator supplying 3.3 V to the sensors; the relay driver (2N7002,
flyback diode); the triac driver's LED, straight from a GPIO at 8–12 mA
drive; the zero-cross input; the Hall sensor (DRV5023, open drain, drawn at
the hand-wheel end of its three-wire cable) with a pull-up and RC filter;
the knee lever, a travel pedal, a pressure pedal (FSR) and a speed-limit
knob into four ADC inputs; a needle-up/down toggle and a single-stitch
button; a heel switch in either pedal (pin 4 of J5, pin 3 of J6, both
4-pin) to one filtered input; and an optional hand-wheel encoder, two
DRV5013 Hall latches (U5, U6) a quarter-cycle apart over a ring of
alternating magnets, on the same cable as the index sensor (five wires:
3V3, GND, index, A, B). The heel and encoder lines reach the board by net
labels (HEEL, HEEL_F, ENC_A, ENC_B).

## Pin use (RP2350B)

| Signal | GPIO | Use |
|---|---|---|
| FIRE | GP4 | gate pulse: timer alarm or PIO |
| RELAY | GP5 | motor-enable relay |
| ZC | GP6 | zero-cross interrupt |
| ENC_A, ENC_B | GP2, GP3 | hand-wheel encoder, PIO quadrature counter (consecutive pins) |
| HALL | GP7 | hand-wheel index (one per turn), edge timestamps |
| STITCH | GP10 | single-stitch button; held, teaches a stop |
| HEEL | GP11 | heel switch in the pedal |
| NEEDLE | GP12 | needle-stop toggle |
| KNEE | GP40 / ADC0 | knee lever |
| PEDAL | GP41 / ADC1 | travel pedal |
| FSR | GP42 / ADC2 | pressure pedal |
| LIMIT | GP43 / ADC3 | speed-limit knob |
| ISENSE | GP44 / ADC4 | motor current (T1, optional) |

ADC5–7 (GP45–47) are spare; GP8/GP9 (UART1) are left for a debug line,
though the USB serial link will usually do. Any GPIO will do for the
digital signals: wire them by GPIO number to wherever the chosen board
brings them out, but avoid the pins boards commonly use themselves: GP0
(the PSRAM chip select, where a board has PSRAM), GP23 (a user button) and
GP25 (the LED, useful as a status light).

**A candidate board: WeAct Studio RP2350B Core (V1.0),** about $6. Its
schematic, 3D model and examples are in WeAct's public repository,
<https://github.com/WeActStudio/WeActStudio.RP2350BCoreBoard>. It
brings every GPIO, GP40–47 included, out to 0.1" headers, along with VBUS,
5V, 3V3, 3V3_EN, RUN and ADC_VREF. From its schematic: GP0 has a 0 Ω link
to an optional PSRAM's chip select, GP23 is the KEY button and GP25 the
LED; and USB VBUS reaches the board's 5V rail through a diode, so PS1 can
feed the 5V pin directly, with USB plugged in as well, and D2 can be left
out. The 3.3 V regulator easily covers the sensors' few milliamps.

A printed stand for soldering the board's pin headers straight is in
`header-stand/`, with a body-bottom (Pico-style) and a body-top variant.

## Firmware outline (after AN1662)

Plain C on the Pico SDK, no RTOS. Each zero-cross interrupt sets a hardware
alarm on the microsecond timer (or starts a small PIO program); at the
firing delay a ~100 µs pulse on FIRE fires the triac for the rest of that half-cycle. The
Hall sensor's period, from edge timestamps, gives the hand-wheel speed. A PI
loop updates the firing delay once per half-cycle (120 Hz on 60 Hz mains),
with a rate limit on the firing angle and a stall watchdog (no Hall pulses
for some time: stop firing, open K1). Fire only after a zero-cross has been
seen and the speed command is zero; close K1 last and open it first.

**Needle position.** With the encoder fitted, a PIO program counts its
quadrature edges in either direction (the Pico SDK's quadrature-encoder
example), and the index pulse sets and checks the count once a turn.
Twelve magnets give 24 counts a turn, 15° each, and the firmware can tell
where the needle is even at rest. Without the encoder, the index alone
gives a position only while the wheel turns (by time since the last
pulse), so stops are rougher and can't be taught by hand. Where the ring
can go on a given machine (on the arm shaft under a removable top, or on
the hand wheel), and what each place costs, is in the motor-control
paper's "Fitting the encoder" (§11).

- **Teaching the stops.** With the needle toggle at "up", turn the hand
  wheel to where the thread releases (the take-up lever at its top, which
  is not quite the needle's highest point) and hold the single-stitch
  button for two seconds (setting top dead center, in the words of modern
  service manuals); set the toggle to "down" and do the same with the
  needle in the fabric. The two angles are kept in flash. They are relative
  to the magnets, so the magnets can go anywhere on the wheel.
- **Stopping.** When the pedal is released, the loop brings the machine
  down to a creep speed and cuts firing short of the stop chosen by the
  toggle, by a learned coast angle; after each stop it reads where the
  needle actually came to rest and corrects the coast angle.
- **Heel tap.** A press and release of the heel switch with the pedal at
  rest sends the needle to its other stop: up to release the fabric, or
  down to pivot. It is ignored while the pedal is pressed.
- A pedal pressed less than its start threshold commands nothing at all:
  no firing, no current, no hum.

## Cautions

- The control side is isolated, so the board's USB port may stay connected
  with the mains on, for UF2 flashing and the serial line; step-through
  debugging needs an SWD probe.
- Never connect a scope's ground to the line side (see the test rig's
  guide).
- Values to check on the bench: R7/R8 set about 3.9 mA peak through the
  H11AA1's LEDs; R11 gives about 8 mA into the MOC3023 from 3.3 V; the
  triac needs a heatsink for long runs.
