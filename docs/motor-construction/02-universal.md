# The universal motor, in iron and copper

## What is inside

A sewing machine's motor, a Singer BZ or BR, or one of the generic
replacements sold for them, is a small two-pole universal motor. Taken apart,
it has four groups of parts.

![A two-pole universal motor, drawn schematically (not to scale): an end
view through the armature. The two field coils are wound on the stator's
poles, and their flux crosses the armature from pole to pole and returns
around the yoke. The armature conductors carry current one way on the left
of the brush axis and the other way on the right (dots and crosses), which
is what makes torque.](../images/motors/universal-end-view.svg){width=85%}

![The same motor in a side view, in section: the laminated armature and the
end turns of its winding, the field iron with its coils, the commutator, two
carbon brushes on springs, the fan, the bearings and the
pulley.](../images/motors/universal-side-view.svg){width=85%}

**The stator (field).**

- **The iron.** A stack of thin steel laminations, insulated from one
  another, with two inward-facing poles. It is laminated because the flux in
  it reverses 120 times a second on 60 Hz mains. A solid iron core would
  carry large eddy currents, circulating in the iron itself, and heat up
  ([Electrical Easy](https://www.electricaleasy.com/2014/02/universal-motor-construction-working.html)).
- **The field coils.** Each pole carries a coil of enameled copper wire,
  wound around its neck, and together the two coils make the magnetic
  field. On many small universal motors the two coils are connected one on
  each side of the armature. The motor's circuit then runs line, field coil,
  brush, armature, brush, field coil, line, which keeps the armature away
  from both supply wires electrically and helps contain the brushes' radio
  noise.

**The armature (rotor).**

- **The core.** A laminated steel cylinder on the shaft, with slots along
  its length.
- **The winding.** Coils of finer wire lie in the slots. Each coil's two
  ends are soldered or welded to two bars of the commutator.

**The commutator and brushes.**

- **The commutator.** A ring of copper bars on the end of the armature,
  insulated from one another with mica.
- **The brushes.** Two blocks of carbon, each pressed against the
  commutator by a spring in a holder. On Singer motors the holders are
  reached from outside, under screw caps, because brushes wear.

The commutator is a rotary switch. As the armature turns, it keeps
reversing the current in each coil just as that coil passes from under one
pole to under the other. The current pattern in the armature therefore
stays fixed in space, flowing one way on one side of the brush axis and the
other way on the other side, while the copper moves through it. Each
conductor in the field feels a force, and every force pushes the same way
around the shaft.

**Everything else.**

- **A fan** on the shaft, because more than half the input power becomes
  heat.
- **Two bearings.** On older motors these are sleeve bearings fed by oil
  wicks.
- **A pulley** for the belt.
- **The housing.** On the 99, the motor bolts to a bracket on the back of
  the machine and drives the hand wheel by a belt. Others, such as the 15-91
  and the 201, have a "potted" motor built into the casting.

## How the parts make up the equation

The speed-control paper's model is

$$v = R\,i + L\frac{di}{dt} + K\,\omega\,i, \qquad T = K\,i^2,$$

and each term has a physical home in the motor.

![The series connection and the lumped model it reduces to. One current
passes through both field coils and the armature, so the field's strength is
set by the same current the armature carries. Below, the three terms of the
model: the copper's resistance, the inductance (mostly the iron-cored field
coils), and the back-EMF.](../images/motors/universal-wiring.svg){width=100%}

- **$R$, the resistance.** This is the copper of both field coils and the
  armature winding, plus the brushes and their contact with the
  commutator. Carbon brushes are deliberately resistive, because commutation
  on AC is harsher than on DC. The resistance limits the circulating current
  in the coil being switched, and so limits sparking. A sewing motor's total
  is typically some tens of ohms.
- **$L$, the inductance.** Most of it is the field coils. They have many
  turns around an iron path with only a small air gap, so a modest current
  makes a lot of flux. The armature contributes some, partly offset by its
  own reaction to the field. $L$ appears only when the current changes, so
  on DC it does nothing in steady running, and on AC it is a large part of
  the impedance.
- **$K\omega i$, the back-EMF.** It is the voltage generated by armature
  conductors cutting the field. It is proportional to how fast they move
  ($\omega$) and to how strong the field is. In a series motor the field is
  made by the same current $i$, so the back-EMF is proportional to both.
  This one fact sets the motor's character:
  - **No current, no back-EMF.** When the current stops, the field
    collapses and the back-EMF goes with it, apart from a few volts from the
    iron's residual magnetism. This is why an MCU cannot simply "listen" to
    a universal motor between triac pulses for its speed (§3).
  - **Torque goes as $i^2$.** Torque is force on conductors carrying $i$ in a
    field made by $i$. When the current reverses, both the field and the
    armature current reverse together, so the torque keeps its direction.
    That is why the motor runs on AC.
- **$K$, the motor constant.** It folds together:
  - the number of armature conductors;
  - the number of field turns;
  - the geometry of the magnetic path.

  It is constant only while the iron is far from saturation. At high current
  (a stall, or a heavy start) the iron saturates, flux stops rising with
  current, and torque grows more slowly than $i^2$.

## Why it runs slower on AC than on DC

On DC, only $R$ and the back-EMF oppose the supply. On AC the inductance adds
a reactance $X = 2\pi f L$, which is about 57 Ω at 60 Hz for the
representative 0.15 H used in this paper. It uses up part of the supply
voltage without doing work. A universal motor therefore runs noticeably
faster on DC of the same voltage than on AC
([Electrical Easy](https://www.electricaleasy.com/2014/02/universal-motor-construction-working.html)).
This is also why a DC drive such as the KBIC, which rectifies the line,
drives a universal motor as a DC series motor (speed-control paper, §8).

## What the nameplates say

Sewing-motor nameplates are approximate, but they agree on the scale:

- **Current:** 0.3–0.6 A on 220–240 V, or about 1 A on 120 V.
- **Speed:** 5,500–7,000 rpm with no load.
- **Power:** outputs of roughly 25–75 W, labeled 1/15 to 1/10 hp, from about
  100 W in.
- **Efficiency:** about 30–40% by one collector's arithmetic
  ([Vintage Sewing Machines blog](https://vintagesewingmachinesblog.wordpress.com/2020/02/01/power-of-a-motor/)).

The motor on the bench here draws about 1.3 A (speed-control paper, §11).
The representative motor used in the simulations below runs at about 7,000
rpm and draws 1.0 A from 120 V, and it delivers a mean torque of about
0.10 N·m, about 76 W.
