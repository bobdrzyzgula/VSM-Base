Hazardous research documentation, not a safe construction or operating guide. The rig is a live bench apparatus: its jack panel, the Wago blocks, the board’s tabs, the decade box, the breadboard and every patch lead can carry mains voltage, and it has not been assessed against any electrical safety standard. It is described here as a tool for measuring motors and studying existing controls, as that work progresses; nothing in this guide makes the KB3151C board, or any other control it is used to study, safe to use on a machine. The rules in §1 are necessary but are not a complete system of risk control. Mains testing. Do not perform the mains experiments described here unless you are qualified to work on mains circuits, are using an appropriately controlled test facility and protective equipment (including residual-current protection: a GFCI in the US), and have had the setup reviewed by an appropriate person. An isolation transformer does not make such work safe.

The rig is a plywood board carrying 3D-printed modules for two jobs:

  1. Characterizing a universal sewing-machine motor: winding resistance, inductance, speed against voltage and current, and torque. These figures feed the speed estimate in the Gen 2 controller’s firmware.
  2. Studying the KB3151C triac board: how firing angle, speed and load respond to the timing resistance, with a decade box in place of the board’s slide pot.

All dimensions are in millimetres unless stated otherwise. Part names in code are the part values in motor_rig.scad.

1. Safety first

With mains connected, these parts are live at 120 V:

  • the triac board, including its heatsink tab (MT2);
  • the Wago blocks;
  • the MT2, MT1, R TIM, MOT A, MOT B, LINE and NEUT sockets;
  • the decade box, whose binding posts sit on the R TIM sockets;
  • the breadboard, if anything on it is wired into the mains side.

Rules:

  1. Change any connection with the rig’s IEC cord unplugged. Plug in last, unplug first.
  2. One source at a time. Never connect the DC bench supply while the IEC cord is plugged in. Mains on the motor terminals will destroy the supply.
  3. The scope goes on the AC side only through the isolation transformer, and then with its ground clip on a single node. The scope’s ground clip is earthed: on an un-isolated rig it shorts whatever it touches to earth. Two probes need their grounds on the same node.
  4. The isolation transformer is not a safety device for your hands. It stops current flowing to earth. It does nothing about touching two live points at once.
  5. Keep the lids on the triac board, the Wago blocks and the breadboard whenever the cord is plugged in.
  6. The decade box must be rated for at least 200 V, and each resistor for at least 0.3 W. Change its setting only with the cord unplugged.
  7. The brake and load stand get hot. Keep each loaded point to 10–20 seconds and let them cool between points.

These rules are necessary, not sufficient. In particular:

  • Residual-current protection. Feed the rig from a GFCI (RCD) outlet. It guards the mains side of the rig. On the isolation transformer’s secondary it sees nothing, because the isolated side has no path to earth for it to detect. The two protect against different faults, and neither protects against touching two live points at once.
  • A floating rig is still live. Through the isolation transformer the rig floats from earth. Anything else connected to it (the scope, a meter, a computer on the scope’s USB port) can pick up its potential, or tie it to earth at an unintended point.
  • Polarity is not known. A two-wire plug can be wired either way, and either leg may be the live one; a ground clip on the “neutral” may be on the line.
  • Procedures. Write down the setup and procedure for each experiment, and have it reviewed by someone qualified, before energizing it.
  1. Strap the motor down before every run. Its starting torque will roll it in the cradle.

Notes on filament choices. Nothing on the rig is loaded anywhere near the strength of any common filament, so the choice comes down to heat, and to which parts carry mains.

  • What the filaments stand. As a rough guide, PLA starts to soften at about 55 °C. PETG holds its shape to about 70 °C (its heat deflection temperature at light load), and ASA and ABS to about 90 °C. PC and PC blends go higher still. Carbon- or glass-filled grades are stiffer and gain a few degrees, but they wear brass nozzles and add nothing the rig needs.
  • The brake jaws: the hottest parts. The brake turns the motor’s output into heat at the pulley, and the jaws hold the pulley through their felt pads. With the duty cycle in §1 (10–20 seconds per loaded point, then a pause to cool), PETG stays well inside its range. Feel the jaws after a few points: if they’re too warm to hold comfortably, wait longer between points. For long runs at heavy loads, print them in ASA or PC.
  • The motor cradle. A sewing motor’s case runs warm rather than hot in short tests, and the felt lining insulates it a little; PETG is enough. A motor run hard for many minutes can get hotter. Then ASA, or simply letting it cool, is the answer.
  • The load pulley and its plunger. Bearing friction warms them only a little. A slipping belt warms the pulley’s groove, so tension the belt rather than letting it slip.
  • The parts near mains. The triac board’s plate and vented cover, the IEC box, the jack panel and the Wago holder carry or cover live parts. The heat is modest: the triac dissipates about a watt at these currents. Use PETG or better, not PLA, so that a warm day or a warm triac can’t let a screw loosen or a cover sag toward a live part. No common filament is flame-rated as printed, so the fused inlet, the lids and the rule of changing connections only with the cord unplugged are what keep these parts safe, not the plastic.
  • The rest. The scale fence, the breadboard tray and lid, and the isolation transformer’s holder see little heat. PLA is fine for the fence and the breadboard parts. The holder is listed as PETG because it bears the transformer’s weight on a bench that may get warm, but PLA would serve.

In short: PETG for everything is a sound default, with ASA or PC for the brake jaws if they will see long, heavy runs.

3.4 Assembly

  1. Mark the plywood. Print rig_template.svg at 100% scale, tiled across sheets. Tape it down, and centre-punch each hole. Drill 3 mm pilots. Lightly round the plywood’s edges.
  2. Mechanical modules.
    • Line the cradle with foam, then screw it down.
    • Screw down the scale fence and the transformer holder.
    • Screw the load rail down with its screw block at the back.
  3. Load stand.
    • Press the two 608 bearings into the pulley.
    • Bolt it to the carriage’s upright: bolt head and nut in the hex pocket on the outside, then the spacer, then the pulley, then a washer and the nyloc nut. Snug the nyloc nut, but don’t preload the bearings.
    • Drop the M5 nut into the carriage’s side pocket. Slide the carriage into the rail and run the tension screw in from the back.
    • Felt the plunger’s end and fit it (spring behind it, if used) into the square bore. Run the M6 drag screw in from the outside until it just touches the plunger.
  4. Triac board.
    • Melt the heat-set insert into the plate’s screw boss and screw the plate down.
    • Desolder VR1 (already done), seat the board over the peg and posts, and fit the M3 screw and washer.
    • Fit the cover last, with the long screws through its ears.
  5. Electrical modules. Screw down the IEC box, the Wago holder, the breadboard tray and the jack panel. Mount the sockets in the panel (red DC+, black DC−).
  6. Permanent wiring. Use 18 AWG stranded wire, 300 V rated or better, with ferrules on the ends going into Wago blocks. See section 4.
  7. Check before first power. With the IEC cord unplugged and nothing patched, check with the ohmmeter that:
    • no socket is connected to PE except PE itself;
    • LINE to NEUT is open.

Scope on C (isolated setup only): clip the ground to MT1.

  • Channel 2 on MOT B shows the motor’s terminal voltage, inverted. Use the scope’s invert function.
  • For current, add a 1 Ω, 5 W resistor in lead 3 and measure across it, with the ground moved to the resistor’s MOT B end. One ground node at a time.

6. Mechanical adjustments

6.1 Prony brake

  1. Pick the side. Run the motor briefly with no load and note its rotation. Fit the brake with its arm on the side where the pulley’s surface moves down, over the scale. To change sides, turn the brake end for end.
  2. Level the arm. Set the scale in the fence, then adjust the M5 contact screw until the arm is level with the jaws seated on the pulley.
  3. Zero the scale with the motor stopped and the arm resting on it. The arm’s own weight drops out of every later reading.
  4. Load it by tightening both wing nuts evenly, a quarter turn at a time.

Torque (N·m) = scale reading (kg) × 9.81 × arm length (m), which is 0.100 for the default 100 mm arm.

The scale. With the 100 mm arm, each gram on the scale is about 1 mN·m. A sewing motor’s 25–75 W at a few thousand rpm is roughly 0.04–0.15 N·m, so most readings fall between about 10 and 200 g, and light loads are a few grams. That calls for a small digital pocket scale (often sold as a jewelry scale), not a kitchen scale:

  • Capacity 500 g to 1 kg. Enough above the readings for the arm’s resting weight and the jolt when the brake grabs; a 100 g or 200 g scale can be overloaded, and an overload can damage its load cell. 1 kg × 0.1 g is a good choice.
  • Resolution 0.1 g. A kitchen scale’s 1 g step is fine at 150 g but 20% of a 5 g reading.
  • Footprint about 75 × 80 mm, or set scale_size in motor_rig.scad to yours so the fence fits it. On a scale whose platform is at one end and display at the other, set scale_contact to the platform’s center too, so the fence puts the platform under the arm. The console says where the arm’s contact screw lands, and warns, with a shorter arm_len to use, if it would land well in front of the platform. Remove or fold back any lid, and check that the contact screw lands near the middle of the platform; a load near a corner reads differently on many scales.
  • Tare, and an auto-off you can disable or a long one. A scale that switches itself off mid-run, and is switched back on with the arm on it, zeroes at the loaded reading and quietly reads low from then on. If it does switch off, lift the arm before switching it on, and zero it again (step 3).
  • Battery-powered, with no charging cable to it while the rig is live.
  • Check it before a session against a known mass: the calibration weight many pocket scales come with, or a US nickel, which is 5.00 g.

6.2 Belt load stand

  1. Slide the motor along the cradle until its pulley groove lines up with the load pulley’s groove (X ≈ 181 on the template).
  2. Fit the belt and tension it with the screw at the back: about 5 mm of deflection under light finger pressure.
  3. Set the drag with the M6 screw. The felt plunger bears on the pulley’s face at 21 mm radius.

The stand gives a repeatable load, rather like the belt drive to a sewing machine. For a torque reading, use the prony brake on the motor pulley instead. The two can’t share the pulley at the same time.

6.3 Tach

Put a strip of reflective tape on the motor pulley’s outer face. Aim the tach along the motor’s axis from beyond the pulley end, where the board is kept clear.

7. Test procedures

Write down the room temperature and the motor’s identity at the start of every session.

7.1 Winding resistance (setup A)

  1. Set the supply to constant current, 0.5 A. Hold the pulley still.
  2. Read the voltage at MOT A / MOT B with the Fluke. R = V / I.
  3. Repeat at 8 rotor positions, turning the pulley a little each time. Record the minimum, maximum and mean.
  4. Repeat right after a 5-minute run to get the hot value.

7.2 Inductance (setup A, scope allowed: the supply floats)

  1. Put a 1 Ω resistor in series and lock the rotor.
  2. In constant-voltage mode, set the voltage that gives about 0.5 A, then switch the supply’s output on. Capture the current rise across the resistor.
  3. Read the time constant τ, the time to reach 63% of the final current. L = τ × (R + 1 Ω).

7.3 DC speed map (setup A)

Step from 5 V to 30 V in 2.5 V steps. Run no load, then at two prony-brake settings.

V I (A) RPM Scale (g) Torque (N·m) E = V − I·R kΦ = E / ω

ω = RPM × 2π / 60. In a series motor kΦ depends on current, so plot kΦ against I. The brake gives a second check: T ≈ kΦ · I. If the torque and back-EMF curves agree, the firmware’s speed estimate rests on solid ground.

7.4 Top-end AC point (setup B)

Record the Kill-A-Watt’s V, A, W and PF, and RPM, with no load. Then E ≈ (P − I²R) / I.

7.5 Triac board sweep (setup C)

  1. Set the decade box, plug in, read, unplug. Repeat.
  2. Use steps of 10 kΩ from 0 to 100 kΩ, then 2–5 kΩ until the motor stops.
  3. Sweep both ways. Coming down from “off”, the motor starts at a lower R than where it stops on the way up: that’s the diac’s snap-on hysteresis. Note both points.
  4. Repeat the sweep with no load, then at one or two fixed brake or drag settings.
R (kΩ) Direction RPM V A W PF Scale (g) Firing angle (°, scope)

For reference, a simple model of the board (0.1 µF timing capacitor, 1 kΩ R2, DB3 diac breaking over at about 32 V, motor effects ignored) predicts:

R box Fires at Motor V (RMS, % of line)
0 13° ~100%
20 kΩ 52° 93%
40 kΩ 77° 80%
60 kΩ 95° 67%
80 kΩ 109° 54%
100 kΩ 123° 41%
120 kΩ 139° 26%
~135 kΩ and up no firing 0

The motor’s inductance and back-EMF will move these numbers. The measured cutoff resistance sets RV2 in the Gen 1 knee controller.

8. Troubleshooting

Symptom Likely cause
Motor doesn’t run in setup C at any R Decade box open or on the wrong range; R TIM leads on pin 3 instead of pins 1 and 2; or a failed diac or triac. The board itself works either way round between MT2 and MT1
Motor runs full speed whatever R is The triac has failed short. Check MT2 to MT1 with the cord unplugged
Brake reading jumps about Felt glazed (scuff it or replace it), jaws not tightened evenly, or the arm touching the fence
Belt slips at light load Tension it, or check the groove alignment
Load-stand drag grabs Plunger binding in its bore; ease the bore, or fit the spring
Kill-A-Watt PF reads oddly in setup C Expected: phase control distorts the current. Use its W reading, not V × A
Scope trace shows a large hum or ground fault Scope used without the isolation transformer, or two grounds on different nodes. Stop and re-patch.
