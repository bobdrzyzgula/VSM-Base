1 Summary

About this paper. The text, analysis, figures and simulations were written by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, at the direction of Robert Drzyzgula, who set its scope and questions. It is a companion to Inside a Switching Circuit (cited here as “the switching paper”) and to Speed Control of Universal Motors in Sewing Machines (“the speed-control paper”). It is a design study: nothing in it has been built or measured yet. The sources are listed in the last section; please report errors as issues on the project’s GitHub repository.

Research use only: not a safe, buildable design. The motor-control circuits, schematics, enclosures, mechanisms, test procedures and software in this project have not been safety-designed, safety-validated, certified, or tested as a complete appliance. None of the controller designs should be built or connected to the mains as drawn, and none should be operated. A concept drawing, simulation, component listing, historical example or commercial component is not evidence of compliance or safety; printed plastic is not an assessed insulation system or fire enclosure, and a barrier drawn on a schematic is not proof of isolation. The work is meant to support understanding of how existing and historical controls work and what hazards they carry, and to guide the development of safer alternatives. Any future buildable design would need a new system- level engineering assessment, independent review, qualified implementation, complete-product testing and applicable certification; docs/VALIDATION.md lists what that would cover. This pedal in particular would put every part of itself at mains potential. Its curves and figures are simulations from assumed component and motor values, not measurements, and are not evidence of performance or safety.

The question. Many vintage machines take a standard cordset: a cord from the wall to a molded block on the machine, and a second cord from the block to the foot pedal. In most of these the pedal is a two-terminal device in series with the motor, as a carbon pile is. The pedal gets no neutral and no earth, and with an unpolarized plug nobody knows which of its two wires is hot. Could a pedal that fits that cordset unchanged sense the foot’s force with a force-sensing resistor (FSR) instead of its travel, and do it with no supply and no microcontroller: a passive circuit, like the KB3151C board but driven by the FSR?

The answer, on paper, is yes, with one open question.

  • The FSR can’t simply replace the KB3151C’s pot. In that board the pot carries nearly the whole line voltage, about 170 V peak, and an FSR is made for a few volts (§2).
  • A clamped timing supply fixes most of that. A resistor from the line feeds a pair of 56 V zeners, which hold the timing network’s supply to ±57 V whatever the line does. The FSR, in series with a fixed resistor, charges the timing capacitor from that. The diac and triac then work as in any pedal (§3). The FSR sees at most about 66 V, and only while it is lightly pressed and carrying well under a milliamp.
  • Simulated, it works like a pedal. The motor starts at about 1.6 N on the sensor, reaches 90% of line voltage at about 14 N, and tops out at 94%. A pedal that passes a fifth of the foot’s force to the sensor puts that between about 8 N and 70 N of foot force: from resting the foot on it to pressing firmly (§4).
  • Two weaknesses the model shows. Like every simple RC pedal, it starts abruptly, at about a quarter of line voltage. And the diac’s tolerance (28 to 36 V) moves the whole curve by a factor of about two, which a trimmer only partly corrects (§4).
  • The open question is the sensor. Interlink rates its FSRs by force and current, and describes 0.1 to 5 V as the typical drive. The circuit’s current is within Interlink’s guidance; its peak voltage is well above the typical drive. Whether an FSR runs for years at 60 V of alternating voltage is something to test, not assume. §6 sets out the test, and what to do if it fails.

What it is not. A passive pedal has no speed regulation, no needle-position stop and no calibration: the MCU controller (speed-control paper, §9) does all of those. This design is the simplest thing that fits a stock cordset and gives a force pedal a better feel than a slide pot and a weak spring.

2 What the pedal has to work with

2.1 Two wires, no earth, unknown polarity

A cordset pedal sits in one leg of the motor circuit. When the pedal is open, the motor is off and the pedal’s two terminals see the whole line voltage between them. When it conducts, the motor runs. The pedal has no other connection, so whatever is inside it must:

  • run from the voltage across itself, as the KB3151C does (switching paper, §6). Before the triac fires in each half-cycle, that is nearly the whole line, because the motor drops almost nothing at the timing network’s few milliamps. After it fires, it is about a volt.
  • be live throughout. Either terminal may be the hot one. Unpolarized plugs leave it to chance, and even cordsets with polarized plugs are wired to the pedal at random: in Robert Drzyzgula’s experience the makers don’t try. Nothing inside is referenced to earth. Every part in the pedal, the sensor and its tail included, has to be treated as being at mains potential. The user is protected by the housing, not by the circuit.
  • let a little current through when it is off. A two-wire pedal can’t open completely, as a carbon pile’s contacts do, because its timing network has to be connected across it. That leakage flows through the motor, a few milliamps, far too little to turn it.

2.2 What an FSR is built for

An FSR is two films: one printed with interleaved silver fingers, the other with a carbon-based ink. Pressing them together shorts the fingers through the ink, and the harder the press, the lower the resistance. Interlink’s integration guides (sources 1 and 2) give the numbers this design depends on:

  • Resistance. More than 1 MΩ unloaded (the X and UX guide gives a stand-off resistance above 10 MΩ), falling to about 1 kΩ at full load. Interlink defines “actuation” as the force that brings it below 100 kΩ. Between actuation and saturation, the resistance follows an inverse power law of force.
  • Range. Base 400 series, 0.2 to 20 N. X series, 0.3 to 50 N. UX series, 0.5 to 150 N.
  • Drive. “Any voltage that suits your circuit is fine. From 0.1 V … to 5 V is the typical range.”
  • Current. Less than 1 mA per square centimeter of applied force: the area actually loaded, not the sensor’s whole area.
  • Repeatability and drift. About ±2% for one part with a repeatable actuator; about 10% hysteresis between loading and unloading; a long-term drift under constant load of less than 4–5% per decade of time.
  • Overload. Keep the pressure below 10 N/mm² of actuator area.
  • Life. Typically beyond a million actuations.

2.3 Why the KB3151C’s pot position won’t do

The KB3151C charges its timing capacitor from the triac’s MT2 through the pot (switching paper, §6). Until the diac fires, the capacitor is below 32 V and MT2 follows the line, so the pot carries the difference: up to about 140 V in the half-cycles where the triac fires late, and the full 170 V peak when it doesn’t fire at all, which is most of the time the pedal is at rest. That is thirty times the FSR’s typical drive, across silver fingers a fraction of a millimeter apart. An FSR dropped in where the pot was would work at first. The question is how long.

There is also a gate problem. The KB3151C drives its BT136-600E through a 1 kΩ resistor, which suits that sensitive-gate triac (10 mA). The snubberless BTA16-600BW the project is buying needs up to 50 mA, so its gate resistor must be smaller (§3).

3 The circuit

3.1 How it works

A clamped timing supply. RD (22 kΩ) runs from the triac’s MT2 side to node N, and two 56 V zeners, back to back, clamp N to MT1. Until the triac fires, MT2 follows the line, so N follows it too until it reaches about 57 V (the zener plus the other’s forward drop), then stays there: a trapezoid instead of a sine (the green trace in §4’s waveform figure). When the triac fires, MT2 drops to about a volt and N collapses with it.

This is the classic way to give a phase-control timing network a steady supply: unijunction-transistor circuits of the 1960s used it (source 6). Here it does two jobs. It bounds the voltage on everything after N, the FSR included. And it makes the timing independent of the line’s exact voltage: a low line no longer means a late firing.

The FSR sets the charging rate. From N, RS (12 kΩ) and the FSR in series charge the timing capacitor C (0.1 µF). With the foot off, the FSR is above 10 MΩ: C barely moves and nothing fires. Pressing lowers the FSR’s resistance, C charges faster, and it reaches the diac’s breakover earlier in each half-cycle, so the triac fires earlier and the motor gets more of the cycle.

RS sets the top end. At full force the FSR falls to about 1 kΩ, so RS is most of the charging resistance there. It limits the FSR’s current, and it decides how quickly the curve approaches full speed: the larger RS, the more of the FSR’s range is spread across the top half of the speed range (§4).

RB sets where the motor starts. RB (100 kΩ plus a 1 MΩ trimmer) across C drains it continuously. C can only reach the diac’s breakover when the charging current outruns RB, so RB sets the smallest force that starts the motor. It also trims out part of the diac’s tolerance (§4).

Firing. When C reaches the DB3’s breakover (32 V nominal, 28 to 36 V across parts), the diac snaps back by about 10 V and dumps that charge into the triac’s gate through RG. With RG at 100 Ω, the pulse peaks near 90 mA for about 10 µs. That comfortably exceeds the BTA16-600BW’s 50 mA maximum gate trigger current. The KB3151C’s 1 kΩ would give about 9 mA, enough for its sensitive-gate BT136-600E but not for a snubberless triac. A diac fires the triac in quadrants I and III only, which is all a snubberless “W” triac needs (switching paper, §3).

Protection. F1 (T2A, slow-blow for the motor’s inrush) is in series with the pedal, so it carries the motor current. The MOV (the 241KD14 the project is buying) sits across the pedal on the inner side of the fuse, so a MOV that fails short blows F1 instead of burning. It clamps surges to about 395 V, well inside the triac’s 600 V.

No snubber. The snubberless BTA16-600BW is rated to commutate an inductive load without one (switching paper, §5). Pads for a 100 Ω resistor and a 47 nF X2 capacitor across the triac are worth leaving on the board in case the bench shows otherwise.

3.3 What it costs at rest

With the foot off, the pedal still passes the clamp’s current. The simulation gives 3.3 mA RMS through the motor, and 0.23 W in RD. The motor won’t turn on 3 mA. RD wants a 1 W part, for margin, and for the voltage: it carries up to about 115 V.

At full force the triac fires at about 49°, the motor gets 94% of the line, and RD dissipates almost nothing, because the voltage across the pedal is gone for most of each half-cycle. The triac itself dissipates about 1.5 W at a 99K’s current, as in any triac pedal; the heatsink already chosen for the knee controller is enough.

4 The circuit simulated

4.1 The model

The figures in this section come from tools/make_fsr_pedal_figures.py, the same kind of half-cycle model the switching paper uses for the KB3151C (its §4 and §6). Its assumptions:

  • The line is 120 V, 60 Hz (170 V peak). Until the triac fires, the pedal sees the whole line; after, about 1 V. The load is resistive, so the triac turns off at each voltage zero. A motor’s inductance carries the current a little past the zero, which this ignores.
  • The clamp is ideal at ±56.7 V.
  • The diac fires at 32 V and drops C to 22 V, as in the switching paper. Its 28–36 V spread is explored separately.
  • The FSR follows a power law, R=100kΩ×(F/Fa)−nR = 100\ \text{k}\Omega \times (F/F_a)^{-n}. For the UX series, Fa=0.5F_a = 0.5 N and n=0.8n = 0.8, which gives about 1 kΩ at 150 N. That curve is an assumption, fitted to Interlink’s stated range and its “about 1 kΩ at full load”; Interlink publishes the shape of the curve, not a formula. The sensors on order will replace it with measurements (§6).
  • Hysteresis. C’s state carries from half-cycle to half-cycle, so the model shows the start and stop forces as the force rises and falls. The FSR’s own hysteresis, about 10%, is not modeled; it adds to what the figures show.
  • “Motor voltage” is the RMS voltage a resistive load would see, as a percentage of the line: a stand-in for speed, which also depends on load.

4.2 One operating point

The figure shows what the clamp buys. Node N never exceeds 57 V, so neither does anything downstream of it. The highest voltage the FSR sees is when it is barely pressed and C swings from one polarity to the other: about 66 V (§4.5).

4.3 Force against speed

With the nominal values (RS = 12 kΩ, RB = 330 kΩ, DB3 at 32 V):

Force on the sensor On a 5:1 pedal
Motor starts 1.6 N, jumping to about 23% 8 N
Motor stops when easing off 1.5 N 7 N
90% of line voltage 13.6 N 68 N
Top 94% at 150 N

Three things to see in the figure:

  • The curve is roughly logarithmic in force. Each doubling of force adds a similar step of speed through the middle of the range, the carbon pile’s virtue the speed-control paper (its §4) wanted to keep. It falls out of the FSR’s power law and the RC timing together, with no shaping circuit.
  • RS spreads or compresses the top. With RS at 6.8 kΩ the curve reaches 90% by 7 N; at 18 kΩ, not until 127 N, and the top is lower (90%). RS is the “how hard do I have to push for full speed” setting.
  • The start is abrupt, and slightly hysteretic. The motor starts at about a quarter of line voltage rather than from zero, and once started keeps going down to a slightly lower force. This is the same snap-on the switching paper (§4) explains for any single-RC pedal: C’s leftover charge from the last firing helps the next one. It is milder here than the KB3151C’s, about 8% in force against the KB3151C’s 15% in resistance, but it is there. A second RC stage, the usual cure in better dimmers, would soften it at the cost of a few more parts and another round of simulation.

4.4 The diac’s tolerance

A DB3 may break over anywhere from 28 to 36 V. Because C charges toward a fixed 57 V, a higher breakover takes disproportionately longer to reach, and the curve moves a long way:

DB3 RB Starts at 90% at
28 V 330 kΩ 1.2 N 7.7 N
32 V 330 kΩ 1.6 N 13.6 N
36 V 330 kΩ 2.2 N 37 N
28 V 100 kΩ 1.5 N 8.3 N
36 V 1.1 MΩ 2.0 N 31 N

The RB trimmer brings the starting force back into line, but it can’t correct the top of the curve, which depends on RS and the breakover together. Two practical answers: measure a few DB3s (a variable supply through a 100 kΩ resistor shows the snap) and keep the ones near 32 V; or make RS a trimmer as well, so each pedal is set at both ends. A higher clamp voltage would make the circuit less sensitive to the diac, but it raises the voltage on the FSR, the one number this design most needs to keep down.

4.5 The stress on the sensor

  • Current. The FSR’s current peaks at 2.5 mA at full force. That is twice what a 402 may carry fully loaded (1.27 cm²), but within the guidance for a 406 pressed through a 20 mm puck (3.1 cm²) or anything larger. This is the main reason the design calls for the 406.
  • Voltage. The voltage is highest, about 62–66 V, when the sensor is barely loaded and its resistance is high, and falls to a few volts at full force. The current at those voltages is under a milliamp, so the power in the sensor is small: tens of milliwatts at most.
  • Polarity. The voltage alternates every half-cycle. That matters, because the failure most feared for silver conductors under voltage, silver migration, is driven by a steady DC voltage across the gap in the presence of moisture. Alternating voltage greatly reduces it, and the pedal is dry.

Even so, 66 V is well above the 5 V Interlink calls typical, and Interlink does not publish a maximum. That is the open question in this design, and §6 proposes how to answer it.

5 The pedal around it

5.1 Dividing the force

A foot resting on a pedal pushes with something like 5 to 10 N, and a firm press is several times that. The simulated curve runs from about 1.6 N to 14 N on the sensor, so the pedal should pass the sensor roughly a fifth of the foot’s force. Two ways to do it:

  • A spring in parallel. The tread rests on a spring that carries most of the load, and presses the sensor through a puck with the rest. The spring also gives the pedal travel and feel: an FSR itself moves about 0.15 mm, so without a spring the pedal is a stiff button. This is the arrangement Interlink recommends for designs that need travel (“use soft rubber or a spring as part of the actuator”).
  • A lever. The tread pivots, and the sensor sits closer to the pivot than the foot does. A 5:1 lever gives the 5:1 division directly. It is simpler, with less travel.

Either way, three things protect the sensor:

  • A puck to spread the load. A rigid disc about 20 mm across with a thin rubber face, pressing on the 406’s center. It sets the loaded area that the current limit depends on (§4.5), keeps the pressure far below Interlink’s 10 N/mm² damage threshold, and makes the response repeatable.
  • A hard stop. The tread should bottom out on the housing before the sensor sees much more than the force for full speed, so a stamp on the pedal can’t crush it.
  • A flat, rigid seat for the sensor, with its vent, at the end of the tail, left open. Interlink warns that a blocked vent makes the response non-repeatable.

The UX’s range helps here. Because it reads to 150 N, the force division needn’t be precise: a pedal that passes a quarter or a third of the foot’s force still stays within the sensor’s range, and RS and RB move the curve to suit.

5.2 Everything is live

Every part of this pedal is at mains potential, the FSR, its tail and the puck’s underside included. The user is protected by the housing alone, as in a carbon-pile pedal, so the housing would have to provide what standards call reinforced insulation. The list below names what that involves; it is not an assessment, and nothing here shows that a printed housing can meet it:

  • Only insulating material where a foot or hand can reach: a printed or molded tread, no metal screws or plates exposed, and fasteners recessed below insulating plugs.
  • An insulating layer between the tread and the sensor. The puck presses on the FSR’s top film. That film is thin polyester, not an insulation barrier, so the puck itself must be plastic, and the tread plastic above it.
  • Creepage and clearance. The spacing between live parts and the outside of the housing, along surfaces and through air, would have to follow what IEC 60335-1 (household appliances) requires for reinforced insulation at 120 V, for the pollution degree, the material’s tracking index (CTI) and the working voltage. Spacing is only part of it. Common printing filament is not a rated insulation material, a printed wall’s layers and voids are not a solid barrier, and a housing would also have to answer for its cord entry and strain relief, fasteners, temperature, flammability, mechanical damage, moisture and cleaning, assembly tolerances and aging. None of that has been assessed.
  • Strain relief on the cord, as on any pedal, so a yank can’t pull live conductors against the housing or out of the board.
  • Heat. About 1.5 W in the triac and 0.2 W in RD, in a closed box under a foot. The clip heatsink and a few cubic inches of air are enough, but the triac shouldn’t sit against the tread.

Because every part is live, nothing inside needs isolating from anything else. That is what lets this design skip the vactrol: the FSR can be wired straight into the circuit, as long as the voltage across it is acceptable (§6).

5.3 Fitting the cordset

The pedal needs only its two terminals, so it fits any cordset that wires the pedal in series with the motor. Most do, the Kenmore’s among them as far as this paper knows, but check before building: some cordsets carry a third conductor to the pedal, for a lamp or a two-speed switch, and that conductor must not be connected to anything in this design. A pedal from the same cordset family, opened and traced, answers it in a minute.

6 Testing, and the open questions

6.1 What testing would involve

Mains testing. Do not perform the mains experiments described here unless you are qualified to work on mains circuits, are using an appropriately controlled test facility and protective equipment (including residual-current protection: a GFCI in the US), and have had the setup reviewed by an appropriate person. An isolation transformer does not make such work safe.

If the design were taken further, testing would go in this order, each step answering one question. The sensor measurements involve no mains; the mains steps are described as research, for a qualified person in a controlled setup, not as instructions:

  1. Measure the sensors. Before any mains wiring, measure each FSR’s resistance against force: the three pre-terminated 402s (base, X and UX) and a UX 406. A kitchen scale makes a good force gauge: put the sensor on the scale, press it through the puck, and read the force from the scale and the resistance from a meter. Ten or fifteen points from actuation to full load give the curve that replaces the model’s assumed one. Rerun tools/make_fsr_pedal_figures.py with the fitted values.
  2. Measure the DB3s. Raise a variable DC supply slowly through a 100 kΩ resistor across each diac and note the voltage where it snaps. Keep the ones near 32 V.
  3. The voltage test on the sensor. This is the test that decides the design (below).
  4. The circuit on the bench, with a lamp. On the isolation transformer (the Hammond 171B), with a 60 to 100 W incandescent bulb as the load. A bulb is close to the model’s resistive load, so the scope should show §4’s waveforms, and the firing angle against force can be checked directly against the simulation. Look at node N, C and the gate pulse.
  5. The motor on the rig. Then the motor test rig, with the motor as the load: check that the triac commutates cleanly without a snubber, and how the start feels with a real motor. A universal motor needs some voltage before it turns at all, which may make the abrupt start less noticeable, or more.
  6. The pedal. Only then the housing, the spring or lever and the puck.

6.2 The voltage test

The open question is whether an FSR tolerates this circuit’s voltage for years: up to 66 V, alternating, at well under a milliamp. Interlink calls 0.1 to 5 V typical but publishes no maximum. The risks are slow ones: silver migration between the fingers, or a change in the ink, either of which would show up as drift or a falling unloaded resistance.

A test that answers it:

  • The setup. Two UX 406s (or a 406 and a spare 402) under fixed weights, one at a light load (where the voltage is highest) and one at rest, unloaded. Drive each from a 60 V AC source through a resistor sized to give the circuit’s current: a small transformer secondary will do, or the rig’s isolation transformer through a divider. Keep a third, identical sensor unpowered, under the same weight, as a control.
  • The measurement. Each day or two, disconnect the drive and measure the resistance at the same load, and the unloaded resistance.
  • The duration. A few weeks is a start: a thousand hours is a respectable soak.
  • Pass. The powered sensors drift no more than the unpowered control, and the unloaded resistance stays above 10 MΩ.

If the sensors pass, design (a) stands as drawn. If they drift, the fallbacks are, in order:

  • Lower the voltage. A lower clamp (back-to-back 43 V zeners, with DB3s selected below 32 V) cuts the sensor’s peak voltage by a quarter, at the cost of more sensitivity to the diac.
  • Move the sensor out of the high-voltage position. Put the FSR across a smaller part of the timing resistance, with a fixed resistor taking most of the voltage. This narrows the force range the sensor controls.
  • Isolate it. Design (b) from the earlier discussion: a vactrol whose photocell sits in the timing network, with the FSR driving its LED at 5 V. It needs a supply in the pedal, which a two-wire pedal has to harvest, and a vactrol whose cell is rated for the voltage it sees.

6.3 Other open questions

  • The abrupt start. Whether a quarter of line voltage at turn-on feels wrong with a real motor, and if so, whether a second RC stage fixes it.
  • The real FSR curve. The model’s power law is a fit to two numbers. The measured curves will move the force window, and RS, RB and the pedal’s force division will move with them.
  • The 402 alternatives. The base and X sensors reach full scale sooner, which would move the window down and make the pedal’s force division larger. The 406’s area still makes it the better choice for the current (§4.5).
  • Temperature. FSRs and diacs both drift with temperature, and a pedal near a heater or in a cold garage will feel different. The model ignores it.
